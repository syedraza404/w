Teaching

I use discussion, case studies, and writing to help students develop their own sociological arguments.

Kenyon College

Visiting Assistant Professor of Sociology

Fall 2026

  • Sociology of Religion

    What makes something count as religion, and who gets to decide? We begin with classical social theory and then turn to questions about secularization, markets, migration, and political movements. In the Case Dossier, students choose a contemporary case, trace the sources they need to understand it, and compare what different theories can explain. They use that comparison to develop and revise their own argument.

  • Contesting India

    How did India become an object of colonial knowledge, economic planning, and competing ideas of national belonging? We follow these questions from colonial classifications and postcolonial development to disputes over caste, secularism, Hindu nationalism, and digital media. In the Category Analysis Essay, students trace the history of a term such as caste or secularism and ask who uses it and to what ends. They build on that work in a case dossier that maps the actors in a controversy and analyzes what is at stake. The course is cross-listed in Sociology and Asian and Middle East Studies.

Planned for Spring 2027

  • Sociology of Science and Technology

    How does a claim become accepted as scientific knowledge, and what gives some people the authority to speak as experts? The course moves from the Scientific Revolution and laboratory research to disputes over public health, environmental risk, and artificial intelligence. For the final case dossier, students map the relationships among experts, institutions, technologies, and affected communities in a case of their choosing. They use that map to ask who has the power to define the problem and what counts as evidence.

  • Institutions and Inequalities

    This introduction to sociology asks how schools, families, workplaces, and markets shape the opportunities people encounter. We consider how inequalities of class, race, and gender persist across generations and how people organize to change them. In the Sociological Imagination Essay, students connect an experience from their own lives to a larger institutional or historical process. For the final case dossier, they use published research and public materials to explain an inequality or institutional practice of their choosing.

  • Sociology of Morality

    How do people come to see an action as right, an obligation as binding, or a way of life as worth defending? We trace how sociologists have studied moral life, from classical debates about freedom and obligation to contemporary research on how people justify their actions and draw boundaries of belonging. In the Moral World Case Dossier, students examine a dispute or practice, reconstruct the moral reasoning of the people involved, and ask how institutions and relations of power sustain that reasoning.

  • Economy, Technology, and Faith in Modern India

    What has it meant to pursue progress in modern India? We explore competing answers through cases ranging from Gandhi's spinning wheel and large dams to call centers and mobile payments. Readings, news coverage, and audiovisual materials help us examine how economic and technological change reshapes work, religious life, and ideas about prosperity. In class presentations, students bring readings or media of their own into the discussion and use them to open up a question for their classmates.

  • Religions of South Asia

    This course explores how religious traditions in South Asia take shape through everyday practices, institutions, and political life. A guiding question is how people decide what counts as a tradition and who has the authority to speak for it. Students consider how histories of migration, political rule, and social change shape the ways people practice religion and understand their belonging to a community.

    Spring 2025
  • Introduction to Urdu Language

    This beginning course introduces Urdu through its script, pronunciation, and basic grammar. Students build a working vocabulary for everyday situations and practice recognizing letters, reading short phrases, and forming simple sentences. The course also situates Urdu in the linguistic and cultural history of South Asia, where it has developed alongside other languages and across religious and national boundaries.

    Fall 2024 and Spring 2025
  • Heritage and History through Humanities

    Who decides what is worth preserving, and how do those decisions shape what we know about the past? This course uses the humanities to examine how communities interpret and pass on their histories. Students consider what cultural works and public accounts of the past can tell us about the people who made them, whose experiences they bring into view, and how their meaning changes over time.

  • Rich and Poor in America

    How does social class shape people's lives, and what makes class inequality persist in the United States? We read theory, history, and ethnography alongside documentaries and fiction to examine how wealth and poverty shape work, education, and family life. In one paper, students use sociological concepts to analyze their own class position. In another, they examine how a film, novel, or other work of fiction portrays class and the institutions that reproduce it.

  • Introduction to the Study of Society

    This introductory course asks how sociologists make sense of familiar experiences. Students learn to connect everyday interactions and choices to the groups, institutions, and norms that shape them. The course introduces ways of asking sociological questions and evaluating evidence about how social patterns arise and persist.

    Fall 2019, Spring and Fall 2023, Fall 2024, Spring 2025, and Spring 2026
  • Sociology of Criminal Justice

    This online course examines how policing, courts, and corrections work in the United States and what happens to people as they move through those institutions. Students consider how policies and institutional decisions shape what happens after an encounter with police, how cases are handled in court, and how punishment is administered. The course asks how we might judge whether the system is meeting its stated aims.

    Summer 2025

Columbia University

  • Core Foundations of Public Health

    This course introduces MPH students to the history, ethics, and human rights foundations of public health. Students consider how social and political conditions have shaped patterns of disease and how ideas about collective responsibility have changed over time. Case examples bring these questions into practice by asking how public health institutions weigh individual autonomy, equitable access, and their obligations to communities.
